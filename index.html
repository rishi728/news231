The Big Story  |  Global Markets

Stocks fall sharply around the world as the AI boom cracks

The Nasdaq fell 5.28% on Friday, Nvidia lost about 14%, and Anthropic's record share sale lost some of its first-day gain.

On Friday 20 November, the Nasdaq index fell 5.28% to 22,617.73. That is a 10% fall for the week and wipes out its 2026 gain. The S&P 500 fell 3.70% and the Dow 1.93%. An index of chip companies dropped 10.7%. Markets in Taiwan, South Korea and Japan each fell 5-6%.

It followed a week of big AI news. Nvidia reported record sales, then faced reports of an SEC investigation and its CEO leaving. Anthropic sold shares at $83 on 18 November. They opened at $111.50 the next day and closed at $97.17, then fell about 9% on Friday to roughly $88-89, still above the sale price. Analysts disagree: BNP called it panic selling, Deutsche Bank called markets oversold.

Research & Discovery

An AI agent designs, builds and tests a circuit board without any person's help

Paper: "JARVIS: An Agent That Designs, Builds, and Tests Circuit Boards on Its Own", by Shibo Zhou, Tianqi Hong, Zheng Yan and He Yang. Posted on arXiv on 19 November 2026 (arXiv:2611.26503). Their institution was not shown on the abstract page. It has not been peer reviewed.

The problem: making power electronics still needs people to design, build and test again and again.

The method: the AI first makes a 3D digital copy of its workbench and practises there. Then, from one written instruction, it runs a robot arm, a circuit printer and a part-placing machine. It checks the board with a simulator and an oscilloscope, and fixes the design if needed.

What they found: a 60 Hz noise filter was finished in 2 hours 39 minutes with no human help, and it cut 60 Hz noise by 50.6 dB. The digital copy caught five problems before anything was made.

Possible uses: automatic prototype labs, small-batch electronics factories, and checking a process in a digital twin first.

Limits (our reading of the abstract; we did not read the full paper): only one simple circuit was shown, there are no failure rates, and it needs specific equipment.

The Idea of the Day

The bullwhip effect: small changes in shop demand become huge swings at factories

Imagine a shop sees sales rise a little, so it orders a bit extra. The warehouse sees that order and adds a safety margin. The factory does the same, and so does its supplier. Each step reacts to the order it receives, not to what customers really bought, so the swing grows as it moves up the chain.

Four things cause it: guessing demand from orders instead of sales, ordering in big batches, sales promotions that pull buying forward, and panic ordering when stock is short. Fixes include sharing sales data along the chain, shortening delivery times and keeping prices steady. You can see the risk in today's news: BAE is raising shell output 16 times, a magnet plant is planned for 2029, and chip rules are rerouting orders.
